## Editorial findings

These are recommendations, not additional changes: your call, your words.

### Highest priority

1. **Evidence versus formal verification.** Under “Text buffers to cloud agents,” the sentence “This has brought formal verification back in vogue” leads directly into tests and reports described as “irrefutable proof.” Tests provide evidence about exercised cases; they do not establish a formal proof of a specification. Either describe an actual proof-backed workflow and cite it, or distinguish verification in the everyday sense from formal verification. The broader claim about renewed popularity also needs a source.
2. **Rex's four “No” entries overstate the evidence.** The comparison marks built-in Markdown, browser, Git and SQLite views as absent. The public Mac action reference does not document these built-in views, but omission is not confirmation of absence. The earlier documentation caveat is no longer in the current post. Safer options: label these cells “Not documented,” or keep the binary format with an explicit note that “No” means not verified in the public docs. Date the comparison and name the build/channel if the values come from hands-on use. Do not count a TUI such as lazygit as a built-in Git view.
3. **“Going from a dozen to hundreds is relatively easy.”** This is the most contestable generalization in the opening section. The rest of the essay explains why supervision, evidence and context become harder. Frame it as a claim about launching sessions rather than managing their work, or bound it to your own experience.

### Argument and structure

- **Opening:** The introductory “unified theory” promises a broad framework; the concrete observation is that supervising agents has made context assembly expensive. Consider opening closer to that observation. No replacement opening was inserted.
- **Repetition:** “Text buffers to cloud agents” states twice that watching an agent work is unproductive, then revisits the same point through reports and context switching. “Bloomberg terminals” repeats the data-analysis and extensibility argument later restated as the superterminal requirements. One cut in each area would bring the reader to the concrete tools sooner.
- **Bloomberg analogy:** The strongest link is the cost of integrating reliable information into a consistent interface. Keep the distinction between Bloomberg's data rights, coverage and network and the terminal UI. Your two plugins are useful evidence of the integration argument; neither proves comparable data coverage.
- **Transition into the product section:** “All the tools and surface area” sounds like the primitives alone complete the system. The next step is the actual integrations. One concrete account of how a plugin changes a decision would support this transition better than another general statement about flexibility.
- **Ending:** The first-person paragraph is a credible return to the opening problem. The final “manager for infinite minds” line shifts from that grounded experience to a slogan. Keeping it is a voice decision, not a spelling issue.

### Clarity and terminology

- **“Central context place”:** Name the actual surface if useful: notes, an agent session, a task board or something else. The phrase currently hides part of the workflow.
- **“Meta-harness”:** Explain whether it is a human-facing control surface, an agent with access to every pane, or both. The product table and plugins could provide a concrete definition.
- **“Hill-climbing task” and “dark software factory”:** Readers outside this discussion may not know what qualifies. A short definition or link would do more work than another general claim.
- **“Because you still are not as involved”:** The sentence about a fully autonomous factory has an unclear comparison. More or less involved than which workflow, and why does that make autonomy a long shot? Clarify the causal claim rather than merely polishing the grammar.
- **Question beginning “When traders live in that world…”:** It contains the main proposed workflow but is very long. Consider separating spawning work, inspecting service metrics and checking tickets into distinct beats.
