# Essay outline: witnessing the change from inside

## Direction selected by Junaid

An approximately 10,000-word personal essay beginning with AI adoption at Atlan, examining the virtue signalling and human flaws around it, and developing Junaid's view of engineers becoming software-factory operators and higher-level decision makers. Return repeatedly to lived experience of rapid change. The apprenticeship seed becomes one part of this larger story.

These are working labels and argumentative prompts, not submission prose. Suggested scenes below are invitations to recover real events; none is asserted to have happened unless supported by the evidence ledger. Evidence IDs J1–J16 refer to `artifacts/journal-evidence-ledger.md`; source IDs refer to `sources.md`.

## Narrative engine

Follow a person whose tools, peers, incentives, and sense of professional worth change faster than he can settle on an explanation. Each major section should change what Junaid or the reader believes. Move repeatedly through an event, Junaid's reaction at the time, the consequence, and his interpretation now.

Candidate argument to earn: the decreasing cost of implementation shifts more engineering work into choosing goals, designing the conditions for agents to work, testing outcomes, and accepting responsibility. It also destabilizes how engineers demonstrate competence. The social response contains genuine learning, fear, ambition, conformity, and performance; the same person can display all of them.

The ending should qualify the forecast. Higher-level decisions do not remove the need for technical depth. Operating a software factory requires the ability to inspect its machinery, recognize failures, and change the process. The essay can make this a direction of travel without claiming every engineer or system has already arrived there.

## 1. The change becomes visible — 600 words

Choose one remembered Atlan event: an onboarding session, demo, review, or conversation in which the change became tangible. Prefer a scene where Junaid had something at stake or made a decision. A successful demo alone is weaker than a demo that changed what somebody expected from an engineer.

- Establish the task, the people’s roles, the available tool, and the surprising result.
- Record what Junaid thought then, before explaining what he thinks now.
- Identify a concrete discrepancy: between apparent completion and actual completion, excitement and uncertainty, or what a person could produce and what they could explain.
- If a discrepancy did not occur, use the real consequence; do not manufacture narrative irony.

Reader question carried forward: how did this become normal so quickly, and what did it do to the people involved?

Evidence: J3–J4 establish preparation and delivery of onboarding, but do not supply a complete scene. Junaid must recover that scene. Avoid a generic industry introduction or invented dialogue.

## 2. What competence used to look like — 750 words

Briefly step back to the earlier job. Describe the concrete work and rewards: understanding an unfamiliar system, implementing a change, debugging a difficult failure, earning trust in review. Select only experiences that explain why the later transformation mattered personally.

Use the 2023 mentoring note (J1) as an honest complication. Junaid already believed engineers needed to understand problems, gather information, make trade-offs, and own decisions. Therefore, the essay cannot pretend these responsibilities were invented by AI. Ask why implementation remained such a visible measure of worth despite that belief.

The 2024 onboarding record (J2) supplies a second anchor: what the company taught new engineers about its stack and culture before the AI-native program.

Turn: a professional identity can depend on work someone already knows is only part of the job. Identify which part Junaid personally enjoyed, valued, or used to judge competence.

## 3. Becoming AI-native at Atlan — 1,100 words

Tell the adoption story through three or four dated events, with consequences between them. Establish what prompted the effort, who acted, what support or constraints existed, and how daily work changed. Recover the earliest relevant event rather than treating the first available journal entry as the organizational beginning.

The currently documented path includes May 2025 preparation, June onboarding and task-force work, SDK instructions, and August review experiments (J3–J8). Tools should appear when they change an action or expectation.

Questions to answer from memory and records:

- What was voluntary, encouraged, expected, or formally required? Do not collapse these distinctions.
- What did Junaid actually help build or teach?
- Which first result persuaded him that something substantial was happening?
- What changed between a demonstration and repeated use on ordinary work?

Include genuine enthusiasm and a real benefit here. Readers need to understand why people wanted the transformation before examining its excesses.

Turn: learning to use AI begins to interact with demonstrating that one is the kind of engineer who uses AI. This requires a specific observed event, not an assumed organizational pattern.

## 4. Learning the tools, performing the identity — 1,200 words

This is the essay's social center. Use two or three observed incidents to investigate what Junaid calls virtue signalling. Define that term through behavior: a visible claim of adoption, sophistication, or commitment whose relationship to useful work was weak or untested. Distinguish it from sincere enthusiasm, ordinary sharing, experimentation, and legitimate requirements.

Possible behaviors to investigate, only if actually witnessed: showcasing a demo without tracking its later use; competing through tool vocabulary; equating heavy usage with impact; dismissing concerns before investigating them; treating an adoption declaration as evidence of competence.

For each incident:

- Describe the observable action and its consequence.
- State what Junaid inferred, and what he could not know about the person's motive.
- Explain which incentive made the action understandable.
- Include his own comparable behavior, mistaken judgment, or reward from the same system.

Give a sincere enthusiast and a thoughtful skeptic room to be right. Avoid casting entire groups as heroes or frauds. Do not invent characters or merge multiple people into an undisclosed composite.

Narrative function: raise the stakes of belonging, competence, ambition, and fear. The same technology can improve the work and become a status marker.

Evidence gap: the existing ledger does not establish specific Atlan virtue-signalling incidents. These need Junaid's recollection and, where possible, corroboration. Personal frustration alone cannot establish someone else's motive.

## 5. The work answers back — 1,000 words

Give the reader a technical episode after the social tension. The strongest available lead is the BugBot review-pyramid reflection and subsequent rules experiment (J7–J8). Recover one real review or task rather than surveying the whole adoption effort.

Show the intended change, what the tool produced or caught, how a person checked it, the missing context or remaining decision, and the actual result. Include both a useful contribution and a limitation if the evidence supports them. A compact code fragment or reconstructed public-safe example can help, but label a reconstruction explicitly.

The historical reflection treats the bot as a learning aid as well as an automation tool. Preserve that: it complicates a simple story of humans losing skill.

Turn: the useful distinction becomes whether a system can reliably complete a kind of work under specified conditions. If deeper review required better rules, show who had to supply the judgment behind those rules.

Do not import unverified hours-saved, PR-count, or percentage claims from generated summaries in the source note.

## 6. My explanation kept expiring — 1,100 words

Make the compressed timeline felt through three revisions of Junaid's mental model. For each, identify a date, his expectation, an event that challenged it, and the resulting change in behavior.

Available anchors: repository guidance and review in mid-2025 (J5–J8); agent-swarm and control-plane ideas later in 2025 (J9–J10); evals, generated-context frustration, and workspace designs in 2026 (J11–J14).

These are evidence of a changing personal model, not automatic proof of an industry-wide transition. Explicitly locate employer or project changes. The later dbt Labs/Fivetran and personal-project experiences must not be narrated as events at Atlan.

Use one dated artifact per revision: an instruction file, a review, a task note, or a design. Avoid a release-history catalogue. Make room for the emotional experience only where it was real: excitement, fatigue, embarrassment at yesterday's certainty, or grief over a familiar craft.

Turn: a more capable agent changes the surrounding work and the explanation of the engineer's role. Junaid begins thinking about the whole production process.

## 7. Building and operating the factory — 1,250 words

Define software factory operationally: a repeatable process that turns an intended change into tested, integrated software, with explicit limits and a responsible human or team. Distinguish a demonstrated workflow from a proposed future design.

Follow one representative change through the process:

1. Choose the problem and define what success would mean.
2. Provide the repository context, constraints, environment, and permitted actions.
3. Have an agent implement within that scope.
4. Gather test results, review findings, and other evidence suited to the risk.
5. Resolve uncertainty, integrate, release, and observe the result.

At every step, identify the decision-maker and a concrete failure mode. Where does the acceptance criterion come from? Who notices that tests express the wrong requirement? What happens when a change is technically correct but unnecessary? Who handles a production failure?

Separate designing the process, operating it, and remaining accountable for outcomes. One engineer may do all three. “Operator” should convey skill and diagnosis rather than merely pressing a start button.

Use J7–J8 for demonstrated review experience. J14 is a design record; it cannot establish that the proposed workspace system works at scale. Existing sources S6 and S9 can frame the metaphor briefly, but the section needs Junaid's specific mechanism.

Turn: implementation capacity can grow while requirements, acceptance criteria, and integration decisions remain scarce or wrong.

## 8. The human problems inside the factory — 1,100 words

Revisit the earlier social incidents through their engineering consequences. This section must advance them, not repeat section 4's critique.

Choose at most three weaknesses supported by real cases. Possible relationships to test:

- Status seeking → visible activity becomes a success metric → more work starts than anyone can evaluate or finish.
- Overconfidence → weak acceptance criteria → a polished result receives insufficient scrutiny.
- Avoidance of disagreement → vague requirements pass through the process → implementation proceeds without resolving the original decision.

These are candidate mechanisms, not findings about Atlan. Include an instance where the technology improved a human behavior, such as making a check cheaper or teaching an unfamiliar failure mode.

J12's rejection of noisy generated plans can support a personal example of learning that additional output was not necessarily additional understanding. It belongs to its actual later context.

Turn: delegating more work makes human judgment consequential in new places. It does not automatically improve that judgment.

## 9. What makes someone an engineer now? — 1,200 words

Develop Junaid's position after the story has earned it. Explore how he now evaluates his own contribution: selecting a worthwhile problem, understanding a system, designing checks, investigating anomalies, communicating uncertainty, and taking responsibility.

Bring back the mentoring note and onboarding experience here. Ask what he would teach differently and what he would keep. Distinguish his proposed curriculum from observed participant outcomes; the latter remain unverified.

Engage the strongest objections:

- If code is where an engineer once acquired judgment, how does a newcomer acquire it now? Supervision alone cannot be assumed to teach understanding.
- Does “higher-level decision maker” conceal an excuse to stop understanding the implementation? Include a case where inspecting code was essential if available.
- Some changes have crisp acceptance tests; others require ambiguous design or risky legacy integration. How far does the factory model travel?
- Engineering already involved decisions and accountability. Specify what changed in their frequency, scope, leverage, or visibility.

State what Junaid still wants to do himself and why: enjoyment, learning, control, or demonstrated necessity may lead to different choices. The position will feel more human if it admits preference as well as efficiency.

Turn: professional worth needs an account that survives changes in who or what produces the implementation. Give that account in Junaid's words, with unresolved questions left visible.

## 10. Return to the opening — 700 words

Return to the opening scene, person, or kind of decision. Explain what Junaid now notices or would do differently. If an actual later encounter exists, use it; otherwise distinguish present reflection from an event.

Resolve the personal tension: what he is willing to delegate, what he wants to understand, and what responsibility he accepts. Prefer one concrete choice over a list of predictions.

Leave one uncertainty that the story has earned, such as how the next generation will acquire technical judgment or whether organizations will reward demonstrated understanding. Avoid claiming the future is settled.

## Keeping 10,000 words readable

- Return to a small set of real people, tasks, and artifacts. Let their meaning change as the narrative develops.
- Place technical detail where it answers a question raised by a scene.
- Use humor from honest discrepancies between expectation and outcome, including Junaid's own certainty. Do not add invented dialogue or ridicule to create entertainment.
- Mark dates selectively so readers can feel the pace. Avoid giving every tool release equal weight.
- Give each scene a consequence. Cut anecdotes that only establish atmosphere or repeat an opinion.
- Alternate lived events with interpretation; avoid several consecutive sections of abstract argument.
- Save the fullest statement of the factory thesis until readers have seen why Junaid arrived there.

## First writing session

Capture six memories in rough personal notes: the first convincing result at Atlan; one observable adoption-status incident; Junaid's own participation or misjudgment; one task where the tool helped; one where human understanding mattered; and a later moment that overturned his explanation.

For each, record approximate date, setting, task, action, consequence, what he believed then, what he believes now, and any supporting artifact. Establish the opening from the strongest of these. If there are no concrete status incidents, reduce that section and let the actual evidence determine the essay's emphasis.
