# Taste Density

I'll read that article first to understand the existing conversation around taste, then help you develop your distinct angle.This is a rich foundation. The article makes the argument that taste is becoming the differentiator as software gets commoditized. Your angle—**taste density** as the organizational equivalent of Netflix's talent density—is a sharper, more actionable framing. Let me help you develop this.

---

## The Core Thesis

Netflix popularized "talent density"—the idea that a high concentration of exceptional performers, without the dilution of mediocre ones, creates compounding returns. Your insight is that in an AI-first world, **the same logic applies to taste**.

When AI can execute at increasingly high levels—write code, generate designs, draft copy—the bottleneck shifts. The question is no longer "can we build this?" but "should we build this, and in what form?" That's a taste question.

---

## What Makes Taste Density Different from Talent Density

**Talent density** optimizes for execution capacity. You want people who can ship, debug, scale, and solve hard problems fast.

**Taste density** optimizes for judgment quality. You want people who know:

- What to build (and what not to)
- When something feels right vs. merely correct
- How to recognize the gap between "works" and "delights"

The shift matters because AI compresses the execution gap. Two teams with different talent levels but similar taste will converge in output quality faster than ever. But two teams with different taste levels will diverge—one shipping forgettable products, the other building things people love.

---

## Why Taste is Hard to Articulate

You mentioned this, and it's worth dwelling on. Taste operates at several levels that resist easy measurement:

**1. It's negative space.** Taste often shows up in what's _absent_—the feature not added, the color not used, the word not written. As the article notes, "none of the tactical things alone _are_ taste; they're mere artifacts or effects of expressing one's taste."

**2. It's time-delayed.** The impact of good taste compounds. A tasteful API decision made in year one prevents a hundred ugly workarounds in year three. A tasteless one accrues technical and experiential debt invisibly.

**3. It's illegible to metrics.** You can A/B test conversion rates. You can't A/B test whether something feels _right_. The Linear landing page doesn't convert better because of some measurable variable—it converts because it communicates a worldview.

**4. It requires confidence without arrogance.** People with taste have strong opinions but hold them provisionally. They're not defending ego; they're defending a vision they can update.

---

## The Apple Lineage as Taste Genealogy

Jobs, Ive, Fadell—you could trace a kind of apostolic succession of taste through early Apple. What's interesting is how taste _transferred_ in that environment:

- **Proximity to uncompromising standards.** Jobs famously rejected work that was "good enough." This wasn't cruelty; it was taste enforcement. People learned what "right" felt like by experiencing repeated rejection of "almost right."
    
- **Cross-pollination across domains.** Ive came from industrial design. Fadell from hardware engineering. Jobs from calligraphy and zen aesthetics. Taste density isn't about hiring clones—it's about hiring people with refined sensibilities in _different_ domains who can challenge each other.
    
- **Shared references.** They could point at the same things and say "that's what we're going for." A Braun radio. A Porsche. The teams with high taste density develop a shared vocabulary of reference points.
    

---

## The AI Amplification Effect

Here's where your timing is perfect. Consider what changes when AI handles execution:

**Before AI:** A team with great taste but limited engineering capacity might lose to a team with mediocre taste but excellent execution. Speed and scale mattered more than refinement.

**After AI:** Execution becomes more abundant. Both teams can ship fast. The taste gap becomes the dominant variable. The team that knows _what_ to build and _how_ it should feel pulls ahead.

This creates an interesting inversion: **taste becomes the rate-limiting factor on quality.** AI can generate ten variations of a landing page in minutes. But someone has to choose. Someone has to say "this one, and here's why, and here's what's still wrong with it."

## Recognizing Taste in the Wild

Your examples are good—Anthropic, Ramp, Telegram, MKBHD. What unites them?

**Anthropic:** The model behavior feels considered. Claude doesn't just answer; there's a philosophy behind how it engages—helpful but not sycophantic, honest about uncertainty, willing to push back. That's taste in AI personality design.

**Ramp:** Finance software that doesn't feel like finance software. They made expense management feel almost pleasant. That required someone to say "corporate software doesn't have to look like it was designed by a committee in 2008."

**MKBHD:** Technical reviews with cinematic production values. He understood that tech content could be _beautiful_, not just informative. And he's been uncompromising about that for over a decade.

The pattern: **someone said "it doesn't have to be this way" and had the taste to envision what it could be instead.**

## Organizational Implications

If you're building a team optimized for taste density, what changes?

**Hiring:** You're not just looking for skills. You're looking for people who have opinions about the craft—who can articulate why something is good, not just that it works. The interview question shifts from "can you build X?" to "show me something you think is exceptionally well-made, and tell me why."

**Culture:** Taste requires psychological safety to express. If people are punished for saying "I think this feels wrong," they'll stop saying it. You need an environment where aesthetic disagreement is welcomed, even encouraged.

**Decision rights:** Who gets to say "no, this isn't good enough"? In high-taste-density organizations, that authority is distributed. Anyone can raise the flag. The default is to investigate the concern, not dismiss it.

**Reference libraries:** Teams with high taste density build shared repositories of "this is what good looks like." Not style guides—inspiration archives. Things they admire across domains.

### From [A personal AI manifesto](../archive/blog-notes/archived/personal-ai-manifesto.md)

The unifying thesis: as AI commoditizes execution, the human edge becomes judgment / taste / agency / protected original thought. A personal AI manifesto is a line-drawing exercise — not "AI is good" or "AI is bad" but: what do I want AI to amplify in me, and what do I refuse to let it erode? The public argument is that personal AI usage needs a philosophy of taste, agency, and craft, not just a stack of tools. Best written as a compact manifesto (a handful of principles), not a long essay.

**What I want AI to amplify in me:**

- Better reading comprehension.
- Better recall and continuity across projects.
- More ambitious drafts, prototypes, and technical explorations.
- Clearer articulation of half-formed thoughts.
- More leverage from the work I am already doing.

**What I refuse to let it erode:**

- Taste.
- Attention.
- The ability to sit with difficulty before asking for help.
- The pleasure of having an original sentence arrive slowly.
- Responsibility for judgment.

**Candidate principles** (each pairs a refusal with an affirmative practice):

- Use AI to extend memory, not outsource attention.
- Use AI to clarify taste, not replace it.
- Use AI to create more drafts, not lower the bar for publishing.
- Use AI to read harder things, not avoid reading.
- Use AI to preserve agency, not become a passenger.
