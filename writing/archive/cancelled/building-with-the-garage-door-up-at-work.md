## Draft v1

There's this idea that floats around the "build in public" crowd on Twitter — share your work, narrate your journey, let people watch you make things. It's solid advice if you're trying to build an audience. But I've never been interested in that game.

What I am interested in: thinking better.

And I've found that the fastest way to sharpen an idea is to expose it to other people before it's ready. Not for the engagement, not for the visibility — but because the moment you write something down for someone else to read, you suddenly notice all the gaps you were glossing over in your head.

### The Problem with Private Thinking

Ideas that live only in your head are too comfortable. Your brain is remarkably good at papering over inconsistencies, at making half-baked concepts feel complete. You think you understand something until you try to explain it.

Remote work makes this worse. No hallway conversations. No whiteboard sessions where someone squints at your diagram and says "wait, what happens here?" Your thoughts marinate in isolation, optimizing for coherence within your own mental model rather than coherence with reality.

The result? You spend weeks on something, finally share it, and immediately realize the foundation was shaky. Expensive lesson. Keeps happening.

### Exposure as a Forcing Function

Here's the mechanism I've started to rely on:

```
Write something half-formed → Share it → Notice gaps → Refine
              ↑                                          |
              └──────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

The key insight is that sharing isn't the goal. Sharing is the tool. It's the forcing function that makes you confront what you actually don't understand.

Andy Matuschak puts it better than I can: if you need to be brutally honest when talking to yourself, you'd better be brutally honest when talking to others about your work. You can't craft polished narratives about your "great progress" without closing your eyes to what's actually happening.

So flip it. Focus publicly on the confusing parts. The frustrating bits. What's not working. This creates a feedback loop that rewards honesty over performance.

### What This Looks Like in Practice

Inside a company, building with the garage door up doesn't mean posting updates to LinkedIn. It means:

- **Weekly updates even when there's "nothing to update."** Struggles and blockers ARE the update. The absence of progress is information. Share it.

- **Rubberduck channels.** Pick a Slack channel and think out loud. Not polished proposals — raw problem decomposition. "Here's what I'm stuck on, here's what I've tried, here's what doesn't make sense yet."

- **In-progress looms, not just finished demos.** Record yourself working through a problem. Let people see the false starts. The backtracking. The "wait, that's wrong" moments.

- **Streams over artifacts.** Instead of sharing the final document, share the messy draft with a note: "This doesn't hold together yet, here's where I need pushback."

The pattern across all of these: you're exposing work while it's still cheap to change direction. Feedback at the messy stage costs nothing. Feedback after you've built the thing costs everything.

### The Mechanism: Why This Actually Works

Two things happen when you share work before it's ready:

First, writing for an audience forces you to surface your assumptions. That thing you "just knew" suddenly needs justification. That step you glossed over needs explaining. The act of making ideas legible to others makes them legible to yourself.

Second, you get feedback when it matters. Most feedback arrives too late — after the architecture is set, after the approach is locked in. By sharing early, you invite course correction when pivoting is trivial.

There's a deeper principle here that I keep coming back to: you expose holes by producing, not by consuming. You can read all the books on system design, but you won't find the gaps in your understanding until you try to design a system and explain it to someone. The hole only becomes visible when you try to empty your head.

### The Trap

Here's where most people go wrong: they turn this into a visibility game.

The moment you start optimizing for how your updates look — impressive progress, clean narratives, strategic framing — you've already lost the plot. You're no longer thinking in public. You're performing in public. Different thing entirely.

The whole point of opening the garage door is to let people see the mess. If you're cleaning up before they arrive, you're just doing marketing with extra steps.

This is the trap I keep seeing. Someone reads about "building in public" and thinks it's a career strategy. They start posting updates calibrated to make them look good. Their manager sees activity, their skip-level sees output, everyone's impressed.

But the thinking doesn't get sharper. The ideas don't compound. Because you're not actually exposing the vulnerable parts — the confusion, the dead ends, the "I have no idea if this is right." You're curating a highlight reel, and highlight reels don't generate feedback that matters.

The goal isn't visibility. The goal is truth-seeking. You open the door so people can wander in and tell you when your table saw is set up wrong — not so they can admire your workshop.

### The Payoff

When you actually commit to this — when you share the half-formed thoughts, the confusion, the blockers — a few things happen:

Your ideas compound faster. They're exposed to friction early, while they're still moldable. Instead of hardening in isolation and cracking on first contact with reality, they get shaped by input from multiple perspectives.

You build trust in a way that polished updates never achieve. People can tell the difference between someone performing competence and someone genuinely working through hard problems. Honesty about what's broken signals that you're actually engaged with the work, not just managing perceptions.

And your thinking improves. Not because you're smarter, but because you've removed the hiding spots. You can't gloss over the gaps when you've already told someone "I don't know how this part works yet." The exposure creates accountability to your own understanding.

The garage door stays open. Not for the audience. For the airflow.
